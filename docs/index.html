Numerical Study of One-Dimensional Quantum Tunneling

Comparison of Crank-Nicolson and Split-Step Fourier Methods

Abstract

We present a comparative numerical study of two methods for solving the one-dimensional time-dependent Schrödinger equation: the Crank-Nicolson (CN) finite-difference scheme and the Split-Step Fourier (SSF) spectral method. Both methods are validated against analytical results for quantum tunneling through a rectangular potential barrier. Our results show that CN achieves higher accuracy for discontinuous potentials, while SSF is consistently faster but suffers from Gibbs oscillations at potential edges.

Physical Model

Governing Equation

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\right]\psi \]

In dimensionless units (\(\hbar = 1\), \(m = 1\)):

\[ i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\right]\psi \]

Initial Condition: Gaussian Wave Packet

\[ \psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{1/4}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{4\sigma^2}\right) \exp(ik_0 x) \]

Potential: Rectangular Barrier

\[ V(x) = \begin{cases} V_0 & \text{for } x \in [x_b, x_b + a] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Numerical Methods

Crank-Nicolson Method

The CN scheme is an implicit finite-difference method, second-order accurate in space and time:

\[ A\psi^{n+1} = B\psi^n \]

where:

\[ A = I + \frac{i\Delta t}{2}H, \quad B = I - \frac{i\Delta t}{2}H \]

Split-Step Fourier Method

Each time step consists of three substeps (Strang splitting):

\[ \psi \leftarrow \psi \cdot e^{-iV\Delta t/2} \] \[ \psi \leftarrow \text{IFFT}\left(\text{FFT}(\psi) \cdot e^{-ik^2\Delta t/2}\right) \] \[ \psi \leftarrow \psi \cdot e^{-iV\Delta t/2} \]

Validation

Both methods conserve total probability with error < 1e-13 over 400 time steps. Analytical transmission coefficient validates numerical results in tunneling regime.

Limitations

• Model is one-dimensional • Non-relativistic quantum mechanics only • Non-interacting particles • Simple rectangular barrier (most experiments) • Finite grid introduces numerical error • Wave packet has energy spread (not truly monochromatic)
